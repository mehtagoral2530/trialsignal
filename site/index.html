      About

      A plain-words guide to clinical trials, watched live.

      Clinical research is public, but it is scattered across the registry record, the journal paper, the company’s announcement and the regulator’s decision. TrialSignal puts the pieces in order so a first-time reader can follow one study from plan to evidence, and keep watching it.

      Who it is for

      • Students and newcomers learning how trial records, papers and industry news connect.
      • Research coordinators and associates getting familiar with a public study and its terms.
      • Clinicians and investigators who want a quick orientation before reading the full record.
      • Biotech, pharma and healthcare teams doing early landscape research.
      • Healthcare product and project professionals studying how research information is used.

      What is live, and how often

      Registry facts come straight from the ClinicalTrials.gov public API while you use the site. There is no TrialSignal server and no account. The registry itself publishes once a day, Monday to Friday, at about 9:00 US Eastern time. TrialSignal checks for that refresh every 60 seconds while a page is open, and again when you come back to the tab.

      Next expected refresh: ….

      Following a trial saves it in your browser. Each check compares its status, main results date, enrollment, posted results and last update with the version you last saw, and flags anything that changed. If ClinicalTrials.gov can’t be reached, the site shows a copy saved on .

      What TrialSignal does, and doesn’t do

      Does

      • Explains public trial records in plain language, one step at a time.
      • Searches ClinicalTrials.gov live and tracks the trials you follow.
      • Links each finding to its paper or announcement and labels how firm it is.
      • Collects selected results, approvals, filings and discontinued programs.
      • Lines up two trials side by side, with gaps marked.

      Doesn’t

      • Decide whether a treatment is right for anyone.
      • Confirm whether a person is eligible for a trial. Only the study team can.
      • Show that one treatment is better than another.
      • Match patients to trials, assess site feasibility or monitor protocol deviations. These were part of the wider concept and are not features of this site.

      Where the information comes from

      Registry recordClinicalTrials.gov: what the study planned, who it includes, what it measures and its current status. Live.
      Company announcementA press release with early headline results, not yet independently reviewed.
      Peer-reviewed paperJournals such as NEJM and JAMA, linked by DOI and checked against Crossref.
      Regulatory decisionAn agency’s review of all the data, such as an FDA approval.

      The plain-language summaries of the library trials were written and checked by hand, last on . The headlines were last reviewed on .

      Not medical advice

      TrialSignal is for learning and research orientation. It does not recommend treatments or trials, and it does not replace clinical judgment or the full source documents. Public records can be incomplete or out of date.

      TrialSignal is an operational efficiency tool. All data must be verified against the official IRB-approved protocol and investigator brochure before clinical application.

      How it was built

      The problem. Anyone trying to understand a clinical trial has to piece it together from a registry record, a journal paper, a press release and a regulatory decision. Each source is written for experts, and new research staff and students often don’t know which source to trust for which question.

      Four versions.

      1. A nine-page dashboard. Every feature had its own page. It showed everything at once and felt built to impress rather than to teach.
      2. Four pages, five steps. Cut to Start, Trials, Updates and About, with each trial read as five plain questions. Calmer, but it read like a document.
      3. Public and live. Rebuilt as a static site that reads ClinicalTrials.gov directly, so anyone with the link gets live search and tracking. The library grew from 10 to 29 trials.
      4. Live first. Feedback was that the site still didn’t feel live. Three competing directions were mocked with real registry data and judged against that feedback. The result: a live headline number, one dark panel style that always means live data, a 14-day activity chart, and motion that only happens when the registry is actually checked.

      Key decisions. Every trial follows the same five questions. Every finding is labelled as a peer-reviewed paper or a company announcement. Registry facts come from the registry, never from hand-copied notes; rebuilding against the live API showed two had drifted. The question box answers from the record and names its source instead of using a chatbot. And there is no server: the API allows direct browser requests, and a weekly job refreshes the saved copy.

      Left out on purpose. Patient matching and eligibility decisions (only a study team can decide), rankings between treatments, and anything that needs private sponsor data.

      Next. Email alerts for followed trials, a readable view of posted results tables, and usability sessions with research coordinators and students.
