How a trial becomes evidence

Every trial passes through the same four stages. TrialSignal shows which stage each finding comes from, so you always know how firm it is.

  1. RegisteredThe plan is posted publicly: the question, who can join and what will be measured.
  2. RunningPeople join and receive the treatment or a comparison while data is collected.
  3. ResultsFirst a short company announcement, later a full peer-reviewed paper.
  4. DecisionRegulators such as the FDA review all the data and decide on approval.

About the project

Why TrialSignal exists

Clinical research is public, but it is scattered. TrialSignal puts the pieces in order so a first-time reader can follow one study from plan to evidence.

The problem

Information about a single trial usually lives in four places: the registry record, the scientific paper, the company’s announcement and the regulator’s decision. Each was written for a different reader, and none of them explains how they fit together.

Finding a trial is only the beginning. A reader still has to work out:

  • Which condition and which people the study is about.
  • What treatment is being tested, and against what.
  • How the study is designed and what it measures.
  • Whether reported results are an early announcement or a full published analysis.
  • What has changed in the field, and how one development relates to another.

TrialSignal organizes information around these questions and keeps the original sources one click away.

Who it is for

  • Students and newcomers learning how trial records, papers and industry news connect.
  • Research coordinators and associates getting familiar with a public study and its terms.
  • Clinicians and investigators who want a quick orientation before reading the full record.
  • Biotech, pharma and healthcare teams doing early landscape research.
  • Healthcare product and project professionals studying how research information is used.

Scope

What TrialSignal does

  • Explains public trial records in plain language, one step at a time.
  • Searches ClinicalTrials.gov live and tracks the trials you follow.
  • Links each finding to its paper or announcement and labels how firm it is.
  • Collects selected results, approvals, filings and discontinued programs.
  • Lines up related trials side by side, with gaps marked.
  • Answers questions from a trial’s record and keeps your notes in your browser.

What it does not do

  • Decide whether a treatment is right for anyone.
  • Confirm whether a person is eligible for a trial. Only the study team can.
  • Show that one treatment is better than another.
  • Replace clinical judgment or the full source documents.
  • Match patients to trials, assess site feasibility or monitor protocol deviations. These were part of the wider concept and are not features of this site.

TrialSignal is an operational efficiency tool. All data must be verified against the official IRB-approved protocol and investigator brochure before clinical application.

Where the information comes from

Each kind of source answers a different question. TrialSignal shows which one a statement comes from.

Registry recordClinicalTrials.gov. What the study planned, who it includes, what it measures and its current status.
Company announcementA press release with early headline results, not yet independently reviewed.
Peer-reviewed paperJournals such as NEJM and JAMA, linked by DOI. Full methods and analysis.
Regulatory decisionFDA announcements: an agency’s review of all the data.

How live tracking works

Search, trial details and status come straight from the ClinicalTrials.gov public API while you use the site. There is no TrialSignal server and no account.

When you follow a trial, it is saved in your browser. TrialSignal re-checks followed trials when you open the site and every minutes while it is open. It flags a trial as changed when its status, main results date, enrollment or results posting changes, or when the record is updated.

If ClinicalTrials.gov can’t be reached, TrialSignal uses a copy of the library records saved on . The plain-language summaries were last reviewed on , and the headlines on .

Limitations. Public records can be incomplete, out of date, or not detailed enough for a specific decision. Plain-language summaries leave things out. Always return to the source.

The problem

Anyone trying to understand a clinical trial has to piece it together from a registry record, a journal paper, a press release and a regulatory decision. Each source is written for experts, and the registry record alone can run to thousands of words. New research staff and students often don’t know which source to trust for which question.

Three versions

  1. Version 1: a nine-page dashboardEvery feature had its own page: dashboard, trial reader, publications, news, comparison, questions, sources, glossary and notes. It showed everything at once and felt built to impress rather than to teach. A first-time reader didn’t know where to start.
  2. Version 2: four pages, five stepsCut to Start, Trials, Updates and About. Each trial became five plain questions. The glossary turned into hover hints and notes moved into a side drawer. A calmer navy and marigold palette replaced the busy layout.
  3. Version 3: public and live for everyoneRebuilt as a self-hosted static site that reads ClinicalTrials.gov directly, so anyone with the link gets live search and tracking. The library grew from 10 to 29 trials across five areas, and registry facts now come from the registry itself instead of hand-copied notes.

Key decisions

Read a trial as five questions

What does it ask, who is it for, what is tested, how is it measured, what was found. Every trial page follows the same order, so the second trial is easier to read than the first.

Label how firm every finding is

A company announcement and a peer-reviewed paper look equally confident in a headline. Each result carries a label for its source, and a four-stage tracker shows where the trial is between registration and a regulatory decision.

Keep registry facts and summaries apart

In version 2, status and dates were copied by hand. Rebuilding against the live registry showed that two of them had drifted: TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 was listed as completed but is still active, and CLARITY AD’s results date had moved. Now plain-language summaries are written by hand, and everything the registry owns comes from the registry.

Answers you can check

The question box answers from the trial’s own record and names the section each answer came from. It is less flexible than a chatbot, but every answer can be traced and nothing is invented.

No server, no accounts

The ClinicalTrials.gov API allows direct requests from the browser, so the site is a set of static files. Followed trials live in the reader’s browser. A saved copy of the library keeps the site useful offline, and a weekly automated job refreshes it.

Left out on purpose

  • Patient matching and eligibility decisions. Getting these wrong could mislead patients, and only a study team can decide who is eligible.
  • Rankings between treatments. Trials differ in participants, length and measures. Side-by-side views carry a clear warning instead of a winner.
  • Site feasibility and protocol-deviation monitoring. These need private, sponsor-level data that a public tool shouldn’t hold.
  • AI-written summaries of live records. The plain-language summaries are written and checked by hand, so they can be trusted for learning.

What I’d build next

  • Email alerts when a followed trial changes, which needs a small server and consent to store an address.
  • A readable view of posted registry results, including adverse event tables.
  • Usability sessions with research coordinators and students to test the five-step reader.
  • A screen-reader audit of the trial page and comparison table.

TrialSignal summarizes public information for learning and research orientation. It is not medical advice.
